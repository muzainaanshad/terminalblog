Last week, a developer posted a simple question on Hacker News: **"What are your career plans in the age of AI?"**

The thread exploded. 13 comments, dozens of upvotes, and a level of honesty you rarely see in LinkedIn thought-leadership posts. No corporate speak. No "AI will augment not replace" talking points. Just developers — junior, senior, engineering managers, founders, retirees — laying out their actual Plan A through Plan E.

Here's what they said, what it means, and what you should take away if you're wondering the same thing.

## The Setup

The OP (akudha) asked plainly:

> "I expect more and more professions getting affected in the next 3 to 5 years, not all on the positive side. Software dev is already changing. What are your plans? Are you planning to quit software altogether? Or stop coding and adapt, accept AI but stay in software field? Or something else?"

They also asked for career level and age range. The responses didn't disappoint.

## The Responses: Raw and Unfiltered

### The Engineering Manager: "March or Die"

**lukasm** (Engineering Manager):

> "This is March or Die scenario. I love building software products, but if I won't be able to do it at any decent company, I'd start my own.
> - micro SaaS
> - consulting for AI solutions
> - robotics or other hardware"

When pressed on SaaS viability:

> "Unless we have a well planned distribution channel, SaaS is tough now that code has no moat - correct? Consulting might still be around at least in the short term, I guess…"

**Takeaway:** Even managers who *hire* developers see the moat evaporating. Distribution > code. The pivot is toward consulting (selling the *application* of AI) or hardware (where atoms resist automation).

### The 12-Year Dev: "Comprehension Debt Will Keep People Employed"

**taurath** (12 years exp, non-FAANG):

> "I think the extent to which companies have pulled out all the stops and sacrificed any order in their systems by pushing devs to vibe up massive amounts of code is a tech debt bill that will come due quickly.
> 
> I think comprehension debt will keep people employed and make for many opportunities. We will be using AI to make refactors, but the reputational risk being taken on by so many companies is something that hasn't been priced in."

**Takeaway:** "Comprehension debt" — code nobody understands because an agent wrote it — is the new technical debt. The bill comes due when something breaks at 2 AM and nobody knows why. That's where humans stay employed: forensic debugging of AI-generated systems.

## The Patterns: What This Means for You

### 1. Distribution > Code > Prompts

The engineering manager nailed it: "code has no moat." The value chain has compressed. Writing code is now the *cheapest* part of software. The expensive parts — distribution, trust, domain expertise, community — are stubbornly human.

**If you're early career:** Stop optimizing for "better code." Optimize for *understanding a domain deeply* and *reaching an audience*.

### 2. The "Order Taker" Role Is Dead

Founder codegeek was explicit: "Anyone who is just an order taker... is going to be eliminated." If your job is "I implement what Jira ticket says," an agent does that faster, cheaper, and without complaining about unclear requirements.

**The pivot:** Become the person who *defines* what gets built. Product sense > coding speed. Architecture judgment > syntax knowledge.

### 3. Comprehension Debt Is the New Job Security

taurath's "comprehension debt" is the most actionable insight. Companies are generating code they don't understand. They *will* need humans to:
- Debug production incidents in unfamiliar codebases
- Refactor agent-spaghetti into maintainable systems
- Explain *why* the system behaves a certain way to stakeholders

**Position yourself as:** "I make AI-generated code understandable, maintainable, and safe."

### 4. Content/Info Products Are First on the Chopping Block

nicbou's 75% traffic loss is the leading indicator. If your value is "I know things and write them down," LLMs already do that better (for the average query).

**The moat shifts to:** Community, trust, curation, *doing* the thing (not just explaining it).

### 5. Have a Plan B, C, D, E — And Make Them Real

mxmpawn's specificity is the model. "Run parents' kindergarten" isn't a joke — it's a researched fallback with a known constraint (demographics). "Buddhist monk" isn't a joke — it's a genuine spiritual off-ramp.

**Exercise:** Write your Plan A through E this weekend. Make each one specific enough to execute on Monday. The act of writing them reduces anxiety more than any meditation app.

## The Uncomfortable Truth

Reading this thread, one realization crystallizes: **The "software engineer" title is splitting.**

Path A: **AI-Augmented Architect** — You define systems, agents write code, you verify/steer. High agency, high leverage, requires deep judgment.

Path B: **AI Operator** — You prompt, copy-paste, deploy. Low agency, commoditized, race to the bottom on price.

Path C: **Domain Expert Who Codes** — You solve problems in biology, finance, logistics, manufacturing. Code is just your tool. AI makes you *more* valuable because you can build solutions yourself.

Path D: **Exit** — Management, consulting, different industry, hobbyist.

Most commenters are consciously choosing or being forced toward A, C, or D. Almost nobody chooses B willingly — but many fear they'll end up there by default.

## What I'm Doing With This

This isn't a "stay positive" post. The anxiety is justified. The displacement is real. The 75% traffic loss, the "welfare mooch" fear, the "last job in the field" resignation — these are data points, not drama.

But the thread also proves: **Developers who think clearly about their leverage survive.** The ones with domain depth, distribution channels, community, or architectural judgment have paths. The ones whose only asset was "I write Python" are the ones panicking.

**My takeaway for this blog:** I'm doubling down on Path C content. Not "how to prompt Claude better" — that's Path B. Instead: how to *architect systems agents can work in*, how to *verify agent output*, how to *build domain-specific tooling* that makes agents reliable in your context.

Because the developers who understand the *system* — not just the syntax — are the ones the founders in this thread are desperate to hire.
