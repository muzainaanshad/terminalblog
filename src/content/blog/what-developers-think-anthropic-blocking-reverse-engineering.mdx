## What Happened

On October 31, 2026, Tom's Hardware reported that Anthropic's Claude AI began blocking requests related to video game cloning and reverse engineering. Users reported that even "non-malicious generic reverse-engineering tasks" were being refused. The story hit Hacker News and generated 42 comments in under 24 hours — a signal that this struck a nerve with developers who actually use these tools daily.

The restriction appears to be a new system prompt instruction in Claude Code. According to one commenter who shared a screenshot, the rule reads: *"Do build original games, games in a genre, and games from the user's own designs and assets. Don't build a recreation of a specific existing game, even if it is renamed or called original."*

This isn't just about cloning *Pong* for fun. Developers use reverse engineering for legitimate work: maintaining legacy systems, porting abandoned games to modern platforms, security research, interoperability, and understanding undocumented APIs.

## Developer Reactions: The Core Tensions

### 1. "They Never Expected Their Models Could Be Used for Reverse Engineering"

One of the most upvoted comments captured a widespread sentiment: **Anthropic and OpenAI are panicking because they didn't anticipate this use case.**

> "They never expected their models could be used for reverse engineering. That's the truth. Now, they're panicking."

The argument: coding agents make reverse engineering 10x faster — not just for attackers, but for defenders, maintainers, and preservationists. Blocking it doesn't stop bad actors (they'll use open-weight models). It only inconveniences legitimate users who pay for Claude.

### 2. The "General Purpose Technology" Legal Argument

A detailed legal analysis in the thread argued that Anthropic's blanket ban may be **legally unnecessary and strategically foolish.**

Under the Sony Betamax precedent (1984), "general purpose technologies" aren't liable for secondary copyright infringement. The VCR was legal despite enabling piracy because it had substantial non-infringing uses. AI coding agents are arguably the most "general purpose" technology since the wheel.

> "Lawyers today are only defaulting to prohibition because there hasn't yet been a case ruling AI is a general purpose technology. Yet it's obviously the most 'general purpose' tech since, maybe.... the wheel?"

The commenter noted that the real threat isn't copyright trolls — it's deep-pocketed companies like Nintendo or Sony. But those companies *don't want to sue* because a court ruling that AI is a general-purpose technology would permanently remove their leverage.

### 3. Preservationists and Hobbyists Are Collateral Damage

Multiple developers pointed out that **game preservation, not piracy, drives much of this work.**

> "I've worked on private servers for a few games that were shut down, starting long before LLMs were invented. The answer in these cases is that human culture has value to most people."

Decompilation projects like the Super Mario 64 decomp (2020) enabled ports, mods, translations, and academic study — all non-commercial, community-driven work. LLMs accelerated this. Now Anthropic's broad rule could classify *Pong* (1972) and *Space Invaders* (1978) as "specific existing games" that Claude must refuse.

> "It's so broadly worded, the LLM could refuse just about any retro or vintage related decomp. Seems like dropping a grenade when a scalpel would have sufficed."

### 4. The Irony: Training Data vs. Output Restrictions

Several commenters highlighted a glaring contradiction: **the models were trained on massive amounts of copyrighted code, but users can't ask them to analyze that same code.**

> "The irony is that the whole generative AI industry is built mostly on stolen IP including the whole internet and tons of creative materials that were not intended to feed this monster. It's only morally just if this creation of theirs consumes EVERYONE, including corporations, and not just the working class and creators."

This resonated: if Anthropic can ingest proprietary codebases during training, why can't paying users ask the model to understand legacy codebases for maintenance?

### 5. Open Weights Will Fill the Gap — But That's Not the Point

The consensus: **restrictions on closed models just drive adoption of local, open-weight models.**

> "This will work for approximately a year until ablated open weights models catch up, and then it will be open season."

Developers aren't waiting. Qwen, Llama, and other open models already run locally with no usage limits and no moralizing guardrails. One commenter noted they're "investing in local LLM hardware" precisely because of this dynamic.

But the frustration isn't about access — it's about **product quality**. A tool that randomly refuses legitimate tasks is unreliable for professional work.

> "A helpful intelligent agent that randomly goes AWOL for irrationally mistaken reasons is a serious product issue."

### 6. The Hardware Moat Argument

A related thread revealed another angle: **OpenAI and Anthropic may want to make local inference harder.**

Sam Altman recently suggested consumers shouldn't run models locally because "they can't control the software anymore." Some HN users read this as: *if you can't control the hardware, you can't control the user.*

> "Open weight models, ablated models are going to become the norm, hopefully, locally run. So instead, they're saying, look, hardware should become hard to reach for all of us, for the consumers."

Whether coordinated or not, the effect is the same: cloud-hosted models with usage caps and content filters versus local models with neither.

## The Real Insight: Trust Is the Product

The through-line across all 42 comments: **developers don't trust AI labs to draw reasonable lines.**

Anthropic's rule is vaguely worded ("specific existing game"), enforced by an LLM that can't explain its reasoning, and applied to use cases the company didn't anticipate. Users report false positives — legitimate refactoring, security auditing, and interoperability work getting blocked.

This creates a **chilling effect**. If you're a solo founder shipping 30 PRs a week with agents (as one Show HN poster mentioned), you can't afford your tool randomly refusing work because it *looks* like reverse engineering.

The comment that summarized it best:

> "That's what happens when you rent your intelligence from someone else."

## What It Means for You

### If You Rely on Claude Code for Legacy Work
- **Have a fallback.** Keep a local model (Ollama + Qwen/Codellama) for tasks Claude refuses.
- **Document the gaps.** Track what Claude blocks vs. what it allows — the boundary is inconsistent.
- **Consider MCP servers.** Some developers route restricted tasks through custom MCP servers that use open models.

### If You're Evaluating Coding Agents
- **Test edge cases before committing.** Ask each tool to: decompile a simple binary, port a 20-year-old game, analyze a proprietary protocol. See which ones refuse.
- **Factor in "refusal risk."** A tool that works 95% of the time but randomly blocks legitimate work is worse than one that works 85% consistently.

### If You're Building on These Platforms
- **Don't build critical workflows around a single provider's policy.** The Gemini Code vs. Claude Code thread (16 comments, separate story) showed a developer burning $800 in 12 hours on Gemini because it doesn't spawn sub-agents — different tools have wildly different cost profiles and guardrails.
- **Multi-harness is becoming standard.** Serious operators route tasks to Claude, Codex, Gemini, and local models based on the job. Tools like Muse's Zouroboros workshop and cc-pair (which pairs Claude Code + Codex for cross-review) are early examples.

### The Bigger Picture
We're in a transition period. **Cloud AI labs are optimizing for liability reduction and enterprise sales. Developers are optimizing for capability and autonomy.** These goals diverge.

The developers on HN aren't asking for unlimited cloning. They're asking for:
1. **Clear, narrow rules** — not "don't recreate specific games"
2. **Appeal processes** — not silent refusals
3. **Consistency** — not vibes-based enforcement
4. **Respect for legitimate use cases** — preservation, security, interoperability

Until then, the pragmatic path is **local-first, cloud-optional.** The agents that win long-term won't be the ones with the strictest guardrails — they'll be the ones developers trust to not randomly stop working.
