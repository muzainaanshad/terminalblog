If you only read the headlines, AI coding agents are magic. They write entire features in minutes. They pass benchmarks. They're "solving coding."

But spend time in the Hacker News threads where actual developers discuss these tools daily, and a different picture emerges. Not the polished demo picture — the messy, contradictory reality of using agents in production.

Three recent HN discussions (with 800+ combined comments) reveal what developers actually experience: models that benchmark better but code worse, solo founders who feel useless because AI does their job, and a fundamental debate about whether "vibe coding" produces maintainable software.

Here's what the conversations actually show.

## The Benchmark Paradox: Better Scores, Worse Code

The most technically specific discussion came from an [Ask HN thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50502344) titled "Is post-training making AI models dumber?" — only 4 comments, but each one hits a nerve.

The OP (CleverLikeAnOx) noticed three specific degradations in Claude after post-training/RLHF:

1. **Agents can't write natural language well** — Claude uses vague phrases like "what the caller calls" instead of "callee"
2. **Agents can't manage encapsulation** — they always produce spaghetti code
3. **Agents are afraid to break anything** — they add workaround code rather than restructuring

"No amount of rules or coaching helps much," the OP wrote. "Ask an agent to make code simpler and it usually gets longer. Add any sort of rule, and the agent will follow it like you just got a wish from the monkey's paw."

A commenter (q17) confirmed the first point with a technical explanation: post-training optimizes weights away from predicting natural language toward solving "binary task completed/not gates." The model learns to check boxes, not communicate.

Another commenter (verdverm) tested multiple model families (Claude, DeepSeek, Kimi, Gemini) and found the problem wasn't universal — Kimi K3.1 handled technical writing well. Their hypothesis: the degradation comes from training models to run "human-free for longer," optimizing for autonomy over clarity.

**The insight:** Benchmarks measure task completion. Real engineering requires communication, restraint, and architectural judgment — exactly what gets optimized away when you reward "finishing the task" above all else.

## When AI Does Your Job, What's Left for You?

A solo founder posted [an Ask HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50495982) that struck a chord: "My product makes money, AI does the coding, and I feel useless."

They built a SaaS in 2019 while working full-time. Now it pays their salary. But: "I miss the days when I was debugging: something was broken, I worked on it, and then it worked because of me. Today most of the code is fixed and AI handles most of what is left. I even wrote the harness for AI to replace me."

The responses reveal the psychological toll that doesn't show up in productivity metrics:

- "I feel the same... what concerns me most is I can feel my brain rotting and losing old skills" (hafern)
- "While AI written code seems compelling and near flawless, it needs human intervention on critical design decisions and edge cases which it can easily ignore. Wearing the hat of a critic and not just agreeing to its design and code suggestions takes a skill in itself" (numbersmith)
- "AI does most of my building too, and what's left for me is saying no. The agents will build anything. Deciding what not to build is still on me" (frankmakesit)

**The insight:** The "human in the loop" isn't a temporary phase — it's the new job description. But the loop shifted from *writing* to *judging*. That's a different skill, and developers aren't being trained for it.

## The Jonathan Blow Thread: 700 Comments on Whether AI Changed Anything

The biggest discussion by far was a [video post](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50484658) of Jonathan Blow (creator of Braid, The Witness) asking: "If AI solved coding, why is my software still the same?"

706 comments. 471 points. The thread devolved into personality debates about Blow himself, but the substantive comments reveal the fault lines in how developers experience AI:

**The skeptics** (like ChrisTrenkamp): "If an LLM generates 100 lines of code for me, I usually end up rewriting around 90 of them... At a certain point you have to ask yourself, if I know what needs to be done and how to do it, why am I spending time arguing with the LLM instead of just writing the code myself?"

They value LLMs for *documentation lookup* — translating novice questions into precise search terms — not code generation.

**The believers** (like dustbunny, a senior game engine engineer): "There are tons of absolutely cracked S Tier game engine engineers who are doing amazing stuff with LLMs... Jon can choose to stay in his shop on the corner hand crafting artisanal ideological computer programs, or he can join us in the frontier."

**The pragmatists** (like Rantenki, building a parametric CAD competitor to SolidWorks): "AI hasn't *solved* coding, but it's reduced friction in ONE part of the development cycle. In most cases, that hasn't increased throughput very much... because it just revealed the next slowest 'rate limiting step.' In cases where there is less overall business friction, and a strong vision without slow moving bureaucracy, it seems clear to me that LLMs *can* greatly increase throughput."

**The architectural critics** (like leecommamichael, summarizing Blow's actual argument): "He's saying modern programmer culture has turned all programming problems into 'leaf functionality' — the stuff which interfaces with OSes, libraries, and web services. He's skeptical that AI can make & maintain a complex system which is correct and performs well."

**The insight:** The disagreement isn't about whether AI writes code. It's about whether *writing code* is the bottleneck. In bureaucratic organizations, it's not. In small teams with clear vision, it is.

## The Education Canary: AI Killed a Game Dev Contest

A smaller but telling signal: [an educational game development program was cancelled](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50502097) because students submitted games made entirely with AI. The only comment: "Same as homeworks."

This isn't about coding agents per se — it's about what happens when the *learning signal* disappears. If students can generate working games without understanding the code, the educational model breaks. The same dynamic threatens junior developer onboarding: if the "grunt work" that taught seniors their craft gets automated, where do the next seniors come from?

## What This Means for You

### If you're a solo founder or small team
The "feeling useless" thread is your reality check. The lever isn't "AI writes code faster" — it's "AI lets you test more ideas with less sunk cost." But you must deliberately practice *architectural judgment* or you'll lose it. One commenter's advice: "I keep doing both, writing some app myself, and some with AI to keep knowledge in my head."

### If you're in a large organization
The Rantenki comment is your roadmap: AI reveals the next bottleneck. If your process has 12 approval gates, faster code generation just means longer queues at gate 3. The teams winning with AI are the ones who also removed the bureaucracy.

### If you're evaluating tools
Ignore benchmarks. The "post-training makes models dumber" thread proves benchmark scores inversely correlate with code quality in specific ways (verbose, indirect, spaghetti, risk-averse). Test tools on *your* codebase with *your* architectural constraints. Watch for: does it restructure, or only add? Does it name things precisely, or vaguely?

### If you're hiring juniors
The education canary is flashing. You can't hire for "writes clean code" anymore — AI does that. You need to hire for "recognizes when clean code is wrong architecture." That's a different interview.

## The Real Pattern

Across all three discussions, one pattern emerges: **AI coding agents shift the bottleneck from *production* to *judgment*.**

Production (typing, scaffolding, boilerplate, lookup) is increasingly solved. Judgment (architecture, restraint, naming, edge cases, saying no) is not — and the current training paradigm actively degrades the model's ability to exercise it.

The developers who thrive aren't the ones who "vibe code" fastest. They're the ones who built explicit harnesses, rules, and review processes to force the agent into the judgment loop — or who kept writing code themselves to keep their judgment sharp.

The tools aren't the variable. The discipline is.
