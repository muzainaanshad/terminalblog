Two Hacker News threads exploded this week. One asked the question everyone's thinking: **"Is anybody producing good code with coding agents?"** The other, titled **"The Four Horsemen of Agentic Coding,"** broke down why the profession feels broken. Together, they pulled 120+ comments from developers who are living this transition daily.

Not journalists. Not thought leaders. Working engineers — senior, junior, team leads, solo founders. Here's what they actually said.

## What Happened

On October 2nd, a senior engineer posted an Ask HN that hit a nerve. They described spending "nearly the whole workday slogging through convoluted code riddled with footguns." Code reviews taking **5x longer**. Drifting further from understanding their own codebase. The proposed solution from the industry: reach "level 4 autonomy" and stop reading or writing code entirely.

That same day, a Substack article titled "The Four Horsemen of Agentic Coding" framed the crisis around four problems: **Slop, Deskilling, Alienation, Accountability.** It argued the tools aren't the problem — how we use them is. The discussion drew 88 comments and 108 points.

The overlap was striking. Same pain. Same split in the profession.

## Developer Reactions: The Split Is Real

### "I Used to Love Learning From My Colleagues"

The original Ask HN poster put it plainly:

> "I used to love learning from my experienced colleagues and taking pride in what we made. We spent time on elegance and craftsmanship. Now I spend nearly the whole workday slogging through convoluted code riddled with footguns."

This wasn't hyperbole. Multiple senior engineers confirmed the same pattern. One described their Slack channels as **ghost towns**:

> "People are logged in, but they're never saying anything. PRs have their names on them, even the review comments are posted from their accounts, but it is obvious that Claude wrote everything — from the code, the unnecessarily verbose change summary, to their comments. Even their replies to my change requests start with 'You're right — …'"

Another developer coined a term that stuck: **"meat proxy."** You ask a colleague a question. They paste AI output back to you. You're not talking to a person. You're talking to a model through a human intermediary.

> "There's a particular sting to being passed raw, unfiltered output from the LLM hose. I thought I was going to be having a conversation with a colleague, but instead my colleague was just playing as a Chinese room."

### The "Two Modes" Workaround

Not everyone is drowning. Several developers described a pragmatic split:

**Mode 1 — "Clankermaxx" (throwaway code):** Frontend React, prototypes, internal tools, visualization dashboards. Let the agent go wild. Don't review. Ship it.

**Mode 2 — Write by hand (real code):** Backend, business logic, infrastructure, anything that matters. One developer put it: "Using Django for backend already removes most of the cruft, and writing by hand also means I actually understand what's going on."

Another described a three-tier system:
- **AI-maintained code:** "IDGAF what the code looks like. I just let Claude or Codex go nuts and 100% vibe code."
- **Code I care about:** "Audit every single hunk as it's produced. Extensive style guidelines. Crack down on 20-line essay comments."
- **Mission critical:** "I write the code myself and have an agent review it."

### The Custom Harness Crowd

A clear pattern emerged: **developers who solved the quality problem built custom tooling.**

One commenter runs a team of ~30 using a custom harness with "level 4 autonomy" — they don't read or write code. Their solution: strong harness, specs-driven development, small PRs. Another built a custom harness on OpenCode v2 with a decision model (Jev) routing easy tasks to cheaper models (DeepSeek) and hard tasks to Opus 5.5. Four tiers. Action fusion. Observation packing. Doctrine injection loading engineering standards into every session.

> "The apps use Flutter and Rust for all of our apps; no ads, no subscriptions. MacroCodex beats everything in its category and it does it for free."

Their advice: **write good specs into files (Markdown). Go through specs with agents. Iterate. Then start development. Garbage in, garbage out.**

Others use cheaper models for review — GLM 5.3 Flash catches mistakes from frontier models at a fraction of the cost. "Don't have Claude review its own code."

### The "Opus 5.5 Solved It" Camp

A vocal minority disagreed with the premise entirely. One commenter stated:

> "By now the code of frontier models works well, and the need to read it is gone. The Claude word salad problem was pretty much solved with the 5.5 Opus release."

They cited DHH, antirez, and steipete — respected engineers who've said they don't read AI-generated code anymore. Another runs a company with large codebases, business-critical software, zero downtime incidents from AI slop, and hasn't written a line of code in over a year. Their "software factory" merges 1/3 of PRs via agents.

> "We still collaborate well. We still care about quality. We still have good knowledge of the codebase. I don't see this as an either/or thing."

The response was sharp: "Really seems more like you got used to their verbal tics and unmonitored quality."

### The Craft vs. Problem-Solving Divide

The most revealing exchange wasn't about tooling. It was about identity.

> "The backlash against agentic coding comes from a split that has already existed among professional software engineers. The ones who are most resistant to it are the ones for whom it has been 'just a job.' The ones who love it are the ones who love solving problems."

Pushback came fast:

> "I got into programming because... I like programming. Not whatever the fuck this is. We've been reduced to hamsters pushing a button and getting a little pellet back, over and over."

Another: "I don't want to hear about the state of your Confluence. I miss discussions and debates about architecture and solutions to problems. I miss the curiosity and learning!"

This divide mirrors what we see in our [Best Coding Agents 2026 Decision Guide](/blog/best-coding-agents-2026-decision-guide/) — teams that invest in custom harnesses and specs-driven workflows ship, while those treating agents as autocomplete extensions drown.

A 40-year veteran weighed in:

> "For over 4 decades I've loved writing every line of code. But now, AI writes most of the code. Instead of coding, I've turned into a full-time reviewer instead. Worst of all, I no longer enjoy the satisfaction of solving hard problems on my own anymore."

### Accountability Proxies

One comment crystallized a darker dynamic:

> "It never does because however you frame it, AI is always the perfect scapegoat. 'Oh, I'm sorry, the bug was introduced by the AI and it made such a good case for it. Damn you Claude, we'll do better next time. I'll add a code-review agent to find regressions.'"

Another called it **"accountability proxies"** — interpersonal opium. Two people in conflict can sink their negativity into a third party (the AI) instead of owning decisions.

This pattern connects to the security risks we covered in [Beware: Hermes Agent Security Audit Uncovers 6 HIGH-Severity Findings](/blog/beware-hermes-agent-oct2026-security-audit-6-high-findings/) — where agents become attack surfaces when workflows lack proper guardrails.

## The Real Insight: It's Not the Models. It's the Workflow.

Reading 120+ comments, one pattern dominates: **developers who treat agents as autocomplete on steroids are drowning. Developers who built workflows around agents are shipping.**

The difference isn't model access. It's:
- **Specs-first development** (Markdown specs → agent iteration → code)
- **Small, reviewable PRs** (not 50k-line dumps)
- **Custom harnesses** with routing, memory, doctrine injection
- **Cheaper models for review** (GLM 5.3 Flash, not Opus)
- **Explicit mode switching** (vibe code vs. hand-written)
- **Team processes** that preserve communication (not ghost Slacks)

The "Four Horsemen" article got this right: the tools expose existing dysfunction. Teams that communicated well use agents to amplify discussion. Teams that didn't... now communicate through meat proxies.

## What It Means for You

**If you're a beginner:** Don't copy the "vibe coding" TikTok demos. Learn to write specs. Learn to review code. Use agents for the 80% (boilerplate, tests, prototypes), but own the 20% (architecture, business logic, security). The engineers who survive this transition are the ones who never stopped understanding their code.

**If you're a senior engineer:** Your value isn't typing speed. It's judgment. The "5x longer code review" complaint is real — but it's a skill issue, not a tool issue. Build review workflows. Use cheaper models as first-pass reviewers. Enforce small PRs. Make specs a team artifact, not a personal notepad.

**If you're a team lead:** The ghost Slack problem is a management failure, not an AI failure. Create spaces where humans discuss architecture *before* agents touch code. Require human-authored design docs for non-trivial changes. Celebrate the "why" discussions, not just the merged PRs.

**If you're building a product:** The custom harness builders are winning. They're not waiting for Cursor or Claude Code to solve their workflow. They're building routers, memory layers, doctrine injection, escalation hooks. The competitive advantage isn't the model — it's the harness.
