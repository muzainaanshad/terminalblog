Two Hacker News discussions exploded this week, and they're telling the same story from opposite angles. One thread has 165 comments about a company that mandated 100% AI coding — a year later, nobody understands the codebase. The other has 12 comments from a 12-year veteran who realizes he hasn't written actual code in a year and is losing his fundamental programming skills.

The pattern is clear: organizations are rushing into AI-first development without understanding the long-term consequences. Developers on the ground are already living those consequences.

## What Happened: The 100% AI Mandate Experiment

A Reddit post (cross-posted to HN) described a company that implemented a "100% AI mandate" roughly a year ago. The policy: all code must be written by AI. Developers became prompt engineers — writing specifications, reviewing diffs, and managing workflows.

The result? A year later, the codebase has ballooned into an unmaintainable mess. Senior developers have quit. The remaining team doesn't understand how their own systems work. When bugs appear in production, nobody can fix them without asking the AI — which often hallucinates the fix.

The HN thread (165 comments) didn't just sympathize. It dissected the failure from multiple angles: technical, organizational, and psychological.

## Developer Reactions: The Comments Tell the Real Story

**On the "manager of AI agents" framing:** One commenter put it bluntly: "Congratulations, you've been 'promoted' to manager." The original poster agreed — the role shifted from writing code to directing AI agents. But unlike human junior developers, these agents don't learn from feedback in a way that builds institutional knowledge.

**On skill atrophy:** A developer with 12 years experience posted: "I just feels I'm starting to lose my basic programming skills to write code from scratch." He described his daily work as "just doing prompt, a review of the generated code, and setting a workflow for agent to specific task." When he interviewed at other companies, he couldn't pass coding tests that require writing from scratch.

**On the "writing is thinking" problem:** A highly-upvoted comment quoted: *"Writing is not merely the final product of thought; it is the primary tool through which thinking happens."* Reading AI-generated diffs feels like understanding, but it isn't. If you didn't write the code, the spec, the tests, or the design — who did the thinking?

**On the fake productivity trap:** Multiple commenters described teams shipping 10x more PRs (500+ per week vs 50 a year ago) while delivering zero real value. "PR output is only increasing... this metric is the only that matters if you want to keep the job." The code is "inefficient and clearly an AI slop" — huge PRs (+260/-60 lines) for minor changes, duplicated features rewritten differently by different developers, nobody knowing how anything actually works.

**On the "wait and see" fallacy:** A debate erupted about whether companies should wait for best practices or dive in. One side argued: "The sane move is to wait and let other people make mistakes." The counter: "Waiting will only ensure you make mistakes others did a year ago." But several veterans noted that the "best practices" keep changing — every model release invalidates previous prompt engineering investments.

**On the senior exodus:** "At this point anyone who was truly passionate about programming, is just less and less motivated to become a better engineer." Senior devs who care about craft are leaving. What remains are people willing to "vibe code" — accept AI output without deep verification.

## The Real Insight: Two Sides of the Same Coin

These threads aren't about different problems. They're about the same problem at different time horizons.

**Short term (the mandate thread):** Organizations that remove human agency from coding get codebases nobody understands. The "100% AI" approach treats code as disposable output rather than a living system that humans must maintain, debug, and evolve.

**Long term (the skills thread):** Developers who stop writing code lose the ability to think in code. The mental models that let you debug a production issue at 3 AM, design a clean architecture, or recognize when the AI is hallucinating — those models are built by writing code, not reviewing it.

The comment that connected both threads: *"In our current zeitgeist, yes it is [normal]. After all, you named it 'software engineering', not 'software masonry'... you make the design, the machine lays the bricks."*

But the mandate companies aren't even doing the design. They're outsourcing the design to the AI too — and discovering that AI doesn't maintain coherent architecture across thousands of commits.

## What It Means for You

**If you're an individual developer:** Don't stop writing code. Use AI as a accelerator for boilerplate, but keep your hands on the keyboard for the hard parts — architecture, tricky algorithms, the 20% of code that determines 80% of system behavior. One commenter's approach: "I try to use it as a learning assistant on my free time and try to find some middle ground for coding (i.e. between ease of coding and understanding what's being created)."

**If you're a team lead:** Mandate AI assistance, not AI replacement. Require that developers can explain any code that ships — if they can't, they own the technical debt. Several commenters suggested: "Use agents to read the code and write a new requirements document from scratch. Iterate on that doc for a long time. Then build the new system." That's AI-assisted understanding, not AI-replaced understanding.

**If you're an executive:** The "10x productivity" metrics are lies. More PRs ≠ more value. One team produced "dozens PRs a year ago, then ~50-100 after claude subscriptions, to 500+ PRs now... there's no value added to product IMO." The only metric that mattered was PR count — Goodhart's Law in action.

**The uncomfortable question nobody wants to ask:** How would you feel driving a car (or flying on a plane, or walking over a bridge) built by developers who haven't written code in a year and don't understand the codebase? As one commenter put it: "Oh we forced AI to use TDD, SOLID and other 'best-practices'? Like that made any difference in all things we did last year."

## The Bottom Line

The HN discussions reveal a crisis that's already happening, not a future risk. Companies that went all-in on AI coding are producing code nobody understands. Developers who stopped coding are losing the skills that make them valuable.

The winning approach isn't "AI does everything" or "AI does nothing." It's the middle ground: AI handles the mechanical drudgery, humans own the thinking. But that requires discipline — and most organizations, under pressure to ship faster, will skip the discipline and pay the price later.

The developers on HN have already paid it. The question is whether you'll learn from their mistakes or repeat them.
